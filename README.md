## Overview

Working landscapes and wildlands operate on deep time. Managing them requires bridging three disciplines that are too often separated: **statutory public land law**, **quantitative ecosystem service valuation**, and **field-level forest and soil diagnostics**.

This repository houses graduate scholarship, legal-ecological synthesis, and site evaluation protocols developed alongside graduate coursework in **Forests and Climate Change** (Master of Natural Resources program, Oregon State University) and applied arboricultural practice.

## Key Synthesis: Full-Cost Ecological Accounting

### 1. From 19th-Century Extraction to Modern Public Trust Stewardship
Under the General Mining Act of 1872 (`30 U.S.C. 22`), the validity of a mineral discovery on federal public lands turns on whether a deposit is "valuable." Administrative and judicial interpretation evolved from the subjective **Prudent Man Rule** (*Castle v. Womble*, 19 L.D. 455 (1894)) to the objective **Marketability Test** (*United States v. Coleman*, 390 U.S. 599 (1968)), which requires demonstrating that a mineral can be extracted, removed, and marketed at a present profit.

However, traditional marketability balance sheets omit unpriced public trust liabilities: acid rock drainage, headwater watershed degradation, loss of old-growth and mature forest carbon sinks, and rhizosphere destruction. Integrating quantitative ecosystem service valuation into administrative marketability determinations aligns 19th-century statutory text with modern federal land mandates under the Federal Land Policy and Management Act of 1976 (`FLPMA`) and the Multiple-Use Sustained-Yield Act (`MUSYA`).

### 2. Heat, Drought, and Edaphic Resilience Across Working Landscapes
Across five years of field observation in Arizona and Central Texas, sustained thermal extremes and vapor pressure deficit (`VPD`) expose a consistent vulnerability: above-ground canopy survival is governed by below-ground pore architecture, mycorrhizal symbiosis, and hydraulic conductivity. Urban and wildland-urban interface (`WUI`) management must shift from reactive canopy removals to proactive subterranean soil and watershed stewardship.
