## 1. Statutory Foundation: The General Mining Act of 1872

Enacted to promote western settlement and mineral development, the General Mining Act of 1872 (`30 U.S.C. 22 et seq.`) declares that "all valuable mineral deposits in lands belonging to the United States" are free and open to exploration and purchase. Because Congress did not define what constitutes a "valuable mineral deposit" within the text of the statute, the Department of the Interior and federal courts constructed a two-part evidentiary standard over the ensuing century.

### A. The Prudent Man Rule (*Castle v. Womble*, 1894)
In *Castle v. Womble*, 19 L.D. 455 (1894), the Department of the Interior established the foundational test for mineral discovery:
> Where minerals have been found and the evidence is of such a character that a person of ordinary prudence would be justified in the further expenditure of his labor and means, with a reasonable prospect of success, in developing a valuable mine, the requirements of the statute have been met.

The Supreme Court repeatedly affirmed this formulation (for example, *Chrisman v. Miller*, 197 U.S. 313 (1905)), grounding claim validity in rational economic expectation rather than mere geological trace presence.

### B. The Marketability Test (*United States v. Coleman*, 1968)
In *United States v. Coleman*, 390 U.S. 599 (1968), the Supreme Court upheld the Secretary of the Interior's requirement that a claimant demonstrate **present marketability at a profit**:
* The Marketability Test operates as a logical complement to the Prudent Man Rule. A person of ordinary prudence would not expend labor and capital to develop a mineral deposit if the extracted commodity cannot be marketed at a price that exceeds the cost of extraction, processing, and transportation.
* Subsequent decisions confirmed that fluctuating market prices, environmental compliance costs, and reclamation obligations directly enter the profitability calculus (*United States v. Kosanke Sand Corp.*, 12 IBLA 282 (1973)).

## 2. The Unpriced Externalities Problem in Public Land Adjudication

While modern administrative adjudication accounts for direct statutory compliance costs (such as surface reclamation bonds and point-source effluent permits under the Clean Water Act), conventional marketability balance sheets treat the surrounding forest, soil, and watershed infrastructure as a zero-cost subsidy.

When hardrock extraction disturbs headwater forests on federal public lands, it imposes measurable economic costs on the public trust:

1. **Watershed Hydrology and Municipal Filtration:**  
   Headwater forests regulate snowpack retention, baseflow timing, and sediment filtration. Removing canopy cover and compacting or excavating soil horizons increases peak runoff and downstream water treatment costs for municipalities and agricultural irrigators.
2. **Forest Carbon Stocks and Rhizosphere Integrity:**  
   Mature forest stands store centuries of accumulated carbon in both above-ground woody biomass and subterranean ectomycorrhizal soil horizons. Surface stripping oxidizes soil organic matter and eliminates ongoing annual carbon sequestration.
3. **Perpetual Remediation Liabilities:**  
   Sulfide ore exposure generates acid mine drainage (`AMD`) that can require active chemical water treatment in perpetuity, shifting long-term balance-sheet liabilities from the private claimant to federal taxpayers.

## 3. Synthesizing the Marketability Test with the Public Trust Doctrine and FLPMA

The Federal Land Policy and Management Act of 1976 (`FLPMA`, `43 U.S.C. 1701 et seq.`) explicitly requires the Secretary of the Interior to take any action necessary to prevent **"unnecessary or undue degradation"** of public lands (`43 U.S.C. 1732(b)`) and mandates that public land management account for long-term ecological, scenic, scientific, and water resource values.

### Policy Framework for Full-Cost Marketability Determinations
Without requiring new congressional legislation to repeal the 1872 Act, federal land management agencies possess administrative authority under `30 U.S.C. 22`, *Coleman*, and `FLPMA` to modernize the Marketability Test's cost ledger:

```text
Net Social Marketability = Gross Mineral Revenue
                         - Direct Extraction, Milling & Transport Costs
                         - Mandatory Statutory Reclamation & Bonding Costs
                         - Quantified Public Trust Ecosystem Service Losses
                           (Watershed Filtration + Carbon Stock Oxidation + Perpetual Water Treatment Risk)
```

1. **Full-Cost Internalization under *Coleman*:**  
   If a mineral deposit is only "profitable" because the claimant externalizes watershed degradation, forest carbon loss, and long-term hydrological risk onto the public, the deposit is not genuinely marketable at a profit.
2. **Replacement-Value Accounting:**  
   Using established federal valuation frameworks (such as USDA Forest Service i-Tree / forest carbon accounting, replacement water-filtration cost models, and actuarial bonding for perpetual water treatment), administrative law judges can require claimants to demonstrate positive net value *after* accounting for public trust resource depletion.
3. **Aligning Prudence with Deep Time:**  
   Redefining the "person of ordinary prudence" to include full ecological cost accounting honors the statutory text of 1872 while protecting the public lands, watersheds, and working forests that sustain rural and downstream communities.
